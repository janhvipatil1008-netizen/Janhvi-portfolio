Product Teardown · MakeMyTrip

MakeMyTrip Myra 2.0: from search-filter-book to conversation-first travel planning

They've made a real architectural bet. But the execution feels built for enterprise trust, not for traveler delight.

📅 May 2026⏱ 2-min summary + full teardown✍️ By Janhvi
Executive Summary — 2 min read
1
Product thesis
MakeMyTrip partnered with OpenAI and launched Myra 2.0 — a fully conversational, voice-first travel planner. The shift from search-filter-book to intent-conversation-transaction is real and correct. But the execution is built for enterprise trust, not traveler delight, so the experience still feels like more work and less return.
2
Target user
Indian travelers planning domestic and international trips — first-time planners to frequent travelers. Core tension: the product is increasingly built for enterprise accounts and travel agents, not the individual traveler using it on mobile.
3
What works
  • Architectural bet is right — conversation-first is the correct direction for travel
  • Voice-first approach addresses a real need — dialects, accents, natural language
  • The map being present is useful — travel is geospatial, having it visible helps
4
What is broken
  • Long blog-type text responses — verbosity kills the experience on mobile
  • AI is trying too hard to please — emojis, over-explanation, sycophantic tone
  • Map is static — the biggest missed opportunity in the whole product
5
My PM bet
Make the map interactive — turn it into the planning surface. Right now the map is decorative. If I could live-plan my route through the map itself — drag stops, see options, build the itinerary spatially — that would actually feel like harnessing LLM power. That's the UX moment that justifies the whole Myra 2.0 bet.
6
Metric I'd track
Conversation-to-booking conversion through Myra 2.0 — how many users who start a Myra conversation complete a booking without dropping to the traditional search-filter flow. That's the real test of whether conversational UX is working.
01 — Context

How I came to this product

I was trying to plan a trip and decided to use MakeMyTrip's new Myra 2.0. I went in as a frequent traveler, not as a product observer — so the experience hits differently. I wasn't analyzing it for a case study while using it. I was genuinely trying to plan something, and the gaps I noticed were the gaps a real user hits.

I also compared it to how ChatGPT handles the same travel planning task. That comparison is useful because it shows what's possible with the same underlying AI technology when the UX decisions are made differently.

02 — Core Bet

The architectural shift: conversation over navigation

MakeMyTrip's original product was: search → filter → listing → checkout. They added Myra 1.0, which was a chatbot layer on top of the same flow — it had search, limited recommendation filtering, and still ended at checkout. That wasn't really a new product. It was the same product with a chat window on the side.

"Myra 2.0 is trying to become a negotiation interface, not a navigation interface. That's the right bet."

Myra 2.0 is genuinely different. The flow is now: intent conversation → agentic orchestration → transaction. You start by telling it what you want in natural language. It figures out what you mean, builds options, and guides you toward a booking. The UI is conversation-first, with voice as a primary input. That's a real architectural change, not just a cosmetic one.

The reason this makes sense for MakeMyTrip specifically is the category. Travel planning is high-anxiety. People are spending real money, making decisions that affect their time off, and dealing with options that are hard to compare. A conversational interface that feels calm, trustworthy, and like it understands what you actually want — that's the right product for this category. The redesign signals they understand this. The execution is still catching up.

03 — User Journey

What the experience actually feels like

You open Myra 2.0 and start typing or speaking. It asks clarifying questions — where are you traveling from, how many days, who's coming, what kind of experience. That part is good. It feels like talking to a travel agent who's actually listening.

Then it responds. And this is where the experience breaks down. Instead of giving you something scannable and visual — cards, a route map, collapsible options — it gives you a long blog-style response. Full paragraphs. Emojis. Detailed explanations nobody asked for. The AI is clearly optimized to seem helpful and thorough, but the output format doesn't match how people actually make travel decisions.

When I compared the same trip planning query on ChatGPT, the response was more visual and structured — day-by-day cards, a breakdown of costs, suggestions organized spatially. It felt easier to process. Not because the underlying intelligence was better, but because the UX decisions were more considered. MakeMyTrip has the same technology. They're making different choices about how to present it.

The map on the right side is genuinely useful. Travel is geospatial — you're thinking about distances, routes, how things connect. Having the map visible while you plan helps orient you. But the map is static. You can see where things are, but you can't do anything with it. You can't drag a route, add a stop, or build your itinerary through the map itself. It's a missed opportunity that sits right in front of you the whole time.

04 — Product Decisions

Five decisions worth examining

Decision 01

Voice-first as a primary input

Putting voice at the center of Myra 2.0 is a real bet. It's not just a microphone icon in the corner — it's positioned as a primary way to interact. This makes sense for the Indian market specifically. People search in Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, mixed-language queries, regional accents. If the voice recognition is genuinely good at understanding dialects and giving back accurate, contextual answers — that's a real moat. The question is whether the comprehension quality is actually there, or whether it's voice input feeding the same text interface.

Right bet for the market · Execution is the question
Decision 02

Trust and calm as design principles

The redesign feels intentional about being calm. Segmented interfaces, clear blobs of information, operational reliability signals. In a high-anxiety category, this is the right call. When someone is booking a ₹50,000 international trip, the last thing they want is an interface that feels chaotic or uncertain. MakeMyTrip is designing for trustworthiness first. That's the right principle. The execution — the long text blocks, the sycophantic tone — works against it in practice.

Right design principle · Execution partially undermines it
Decision 03

The text response format — a clear UX mistake

The response format is the biggest problem I noticed. Long paragraphs, emojis, over-explanation. Travel planning involves a lot of visual and spatial processing — you're comparing options, imagining places, thinking about routes. That kind of decision-making needs cards, visuals, maps, and collapsible sections — not essays. ChatGPT's travel planning response is better structured not because it's smarter, but because someone thought harder about how people actually process travel information.

Biggest UX gap · Wrong format for the decision type
Decision 04

The static map — present but passive

Having the map visible is genuinely useful. It helps you stay oriented spatially while you're planning. But it's static. You can see hotel locations and city markers. You can't plan through it. You can't drag your route, add stops, or build an itinerary spatially. For a geospatial category like travel, the map should be the primary planning surface — not a sidebar decoration.

Useful but passive · Biggest missed opportunity in the product
Decision 05

Built for enterprise, not for the traveler

This is a consumer app, but it's clear that the product decisions are being made for enterprise customers — corporate travel accounts, travel agents, B2B clients. The interface is reliable and legible, but it lacks the energy and delight that a traveler planning a personal trip actually wants. When I'm booking a trip, I'm also excited. I want to feel the dopamine hit of seeing a beautiful destination, exploring options, imagining the experience. The current interface feels like a business tool that happens to also work for personal travel — not the other way around.

Consumer app built for enterprise · Traveler experience suffers
05 — Competitors

What MakeMyTrip is up against

Google Travel and Google Flights remain the default for most people starting travel research because the information density is high and the visual structure is good. Booking.com and Airbnb have stronger visual experiences for accommodation. In the AI travel space, startups like Roam Around and Layla are building AI-first travel planners from scratch — no legacy booking infrastructure to work around, which means they can make faster UX decisions. The risk for MakeMyTrip is being stuck in the middle: too legacy to move as fast as AI-native startups, too consumer-facing to win purely on enterprise reliability. Myra 2.0 is a real attempt to escape that trap. Whether it succeeds depends on closing the gap between the architectural bet and the execution.

06 — What I'd Do Next

Three bets — with priority

Bet 01
P0

Reformat AI responses — cards, visuals, collapsible sections

ProblemLong paragraph responses don't match how people make travel decisions. The format creates cognitive load instead of reducing it.
Why nowThis is the most visible UX failure in the current product. Every traveler who hits this response format and compares it to a ChatGPT or Google result will feel the gap immediately.
ShipStructured response format for travel queries: day-by-day cards, cost breakdown table, destination images, collapsible "tell me more" sections. The same information, in a format that matches how people actually process travel choices.
MeasureConversation-to-booking conversion rate before and after. Session depth — do users keep planning or drop off after the first response?
Tradeoff: Structured responses are harder to generate reliably than free-form text. Requires more prompt engineering and output validation. P0 because it's the most immediate impact on the user experience.
Bet 02
P1

Make the map interactive — live route planning on the map

ProblemThe map is present but passive. Travel is inherently geospatial. Users want to plan through the map, not alongside it.
Why nowThis is the feature that would actually justify the "AI-powered travel planner" positioning. Right now the map doesn't do that. Making it interactive would create a genuinely differentiated experience no competitor has.
ShipA live route planning layer on the existing map. Users can drag stops, add destinations, reorder the itinerary spatially. Myra responds to map changes in real time — "you've added Coorg to Day 2, here are stay options near the route." The conversation and the map are synced.
MeasureMap interaction rate during planning sessions. Multi-destination booking rate among users who use the interactive map vs. those who don't.
Tradeoff: Technically complex — syncing the conversational AI with real-time map interactions requires solid engineering. P1 because it's the highest-differentiation move but requires more build time than a response format change.
Bet 03
P2

Add traveler delight signals — images, dopamine, excitement

ProblemThe current experience is calm and reliable but not exciting. When someone is planning a personal trip, they're emotionally invested. The interface doesn't match that energy.
Why nowBooking.com and Airbnb have shown that visual richness drives conversion in travel. The emotional experience of imagining a destination is part of the booking decision.
ShipHigh-quality destination images surfaced proactively during the planning conversation. "You mentioned Coorg — here's what the coffee estates look like in October." Visual moments that build excitement while building the trip.
MeasureClick-through rate on destination images. Correlation between image engagement and final booking. NPS among personal travel users vs. corporate travel users.
Tradeoff: Risk of making the interface feel cluttered if images aren't surfaced contextually. P2 because it's a nice-to-have until the core response format and map issues are fixed — delight on a broken foundation doesn't work.
07 — Bigger Pattern

What MakeMyTrip teaches about AI in consumer products

The technology is not the hard part anymore. MakeMyTrip has access to OpenAI's models. They have the same capabilities as any AI-native startup. The gap between what's possible and what's shipped isn't a technology gap — it's a UX and product decision gap. We know LLMs can do a lot. The question is what decisions you make about how to present that capability to a user.

Moving the needle means delivering value to the actual user, not just to the business. Myra 2.0 feels like it was designed to satisfy enterprise clients and demonstrate AI capability to stakeholders. That's useful, but it's not what drives retention and word-of-mouth among real travelers. The products that win in consumer AI are the ones that make the user feel something — excitement, confidence, clarity. MakeMyTrip has the infrastructure, the data, and now the AI. What it needs is a clearer commitment to the traveler's experience over the enterprise client's requirements.

Product Teardown · Claude

Claude: from AI chatbot to AI workspace — and the cost of getting there

The limits and cost hit hardest exactly when Claude is working best — that's the central tension of the product right now.

📅 May 2026⏱ 2-min summary + full teardown✍️ By Janhvi
Executive Summary — 2 min read
1
Product thesis
Claude is not really a chatbot anymore. The product is moving deliberately toward becoming an AI workspace for people who do serious work — Plan Mode, Projects, Artifacts, MCP, extended thinking all point the same way. But the limits and cost hit hardest exactly when Claude is working best.
2
Target user
Builders, researchers, and technical PMs doing serious ongoing work — API developers, AI product teams, researchers. Not casual chatters. Claude's best users are its most constrained ones.
3
What works
  • Plan Mode — reasoning before output, best coding UX I've used
  • Projects — persistent context that makes serious ongoing work possible
  • MCP connectivity — turns Claude into an intelligence layer, not just a chatbot
4
What is broken
  • Limits appear mid-flow — exactly when you're most dependent on it
  • Cost scales with depth — users who get most value hit limits first
  • Artifacts don't persist in Projects — workspace experience is incomplete
5
My PM bet
Usage visibility before the wall — not after it. Right now limits appear as an abrupt interruption mid-work. A lightweight contextual signal — "~60% of your daily limit used in this session" — gives users a chance to wrap up before the wall hits. Every other serious workspace tool shows you capacity before you hit it.
6
Metric I'd track
Weekly active Projects — users who return to a persistent project context. This measures whether Claude is becoming a workspace (sticky, ongoing) vs. a chatbot (transactional, one-shot).
01 — Core Bet

Workspace over chatbot — a deliberate product thesis

When I look at what Anthropic has actually shipped — Projects, Artifacts, extended thinking, Skills, MCP connectivity, the API surface — I see a product moving away from the chat paradigm toward something more like a persistent AI workspace. Projects give persistent context. Artifacts let you build inside the conversation. MCP pulls in real tools and data. Extended thinking trades speed for depth. None of these are chat features. They're workspace features.

"Claude is not trying to give you the fastest answer. It's trying to give you the right environment to do serious work."

What Anthropic is betting on is that the users who matter most don't want a faster chatbot. They want a better thinking and building environment. Every feature shipped in the last year is consistent with that bet.

02 — User Journey

How serious users actually experience Claude

A builder's Claude journey: set up a Project with system context and relevant files, start a session, use Plan Mode to think through architecture before writing code, use extended thinking for hard design decisions, pull in MCP data when needed, build an Artifact to preview output. This is qualitatively different from "open chat, ask question, get answer."

The break point comes mid-session. You're deep in a complex task — the AI has full context, it's reasoning well, you're making progress — and the limit hits. The conversation ends. Context is gone. You're starting over or summarizing manually. This moment is the single biggest product failure in Claude right now, because it hits the users who've invested the most in the workspace setup.

The casual user barely notices limits. The power user — the exact person the workspace bet is designed to serve — hits them hardest and most painfully. That structural inversion is what makes this worth writing about.

03 — Product Decisions

Five decisions that show what Claude is becoming

Decision 01

Plan Mode — reasoning-first coding

Before writing code, Claude shows the implementation direction. I can catch wrong assumptions before they cascade. Redirect before the work is done. This is fundamentally different from "here's the code, good luck." It treats me like a collaborator. The most underrated feature in Claude — and the clearest expression of the workspace thesis applied to coding.

Reasoning before output
Decision 02

Projects — persistent context as product identity

When I set up a Project with my AgriSathi context — system architecture, user research notes, current sprint — every conversation starts with the full picture. I don't re-explain the system every session. For ongoing work — building a product, researching over weeks — this is the feature that makes Claude feel like a workspace rather than a reset-every-session tool.

Stickiness through context
Decision 03

MCP — intelligence layer, not just a chatbot

I've connected Claude to MCPs for tool-based workflows and it changes what the product feels like entirely. Instead of Claude being the thing you talk to, it becomes the intelligence layer inside a larger system — pulling real data, taking real actions. This is a platform bet. The more tools you connect, the more powerful it becomes.

Platform signal · Extensibility creates stickiness
Decision 04

Artifacts — output you can use, not just read

Artifacts change the output format from text to something interactive — a component lives in a preview panel, not a code block. The limitation is that Artifacts don't persist meaningfully in Projects across sessions. The workspace context survives. The work output doesn't. This is the most obvious gap in the product right now.

Gap · Artifacts and Projects need to connect
Decision 05

Extended thinking — depth over speed, explicitly

Extended thinking trades response speed for reasoning depth — explicitly, as a user choice. It takes longer, uses more tokens, costs more. For architectural decisions, complex research, multi-step system design — it's noticeably better. Shipping this as an explicit toggle tells you who Anthropic thinks their user is: someone who knows the difference between a fast answer and a well-reasoned one.

Depth as a product value
04 — Competitors

Who's competing for the same serious user

ChatGPT with memory and Canvas is the most direct competitor — similar surface area, larger user base, stronger brand recognition among casual users. Cursor and GitHub Copilot compete specifically on the coding side, where Plan Mode is Claude's strongest differentiator. Gemini with its 1M+ context window and Google Workspace integration competes on the enterprise and research side. The interesting dynamic is that Claude is competing on depth while most competitors compete on breadth — a deliberate bet that the serious user market is worth owning, even at the cost of the casual user market.

05 — What I'd Do Next

Three bets — with priority

Bet 01
P0

Usage visibility before the wall — not after it

ProblemLimits appear as abrupt interruptions mid-work. No warning, no signal, no chance to adjust. Breaks the workspace feeling entirely.
Why nowThis is the #1 complaint from power users — the exact users the workspace bet depends on retaining.
ShipA lightweight session usage indicator: "~60% of your daily limit used." Contextual, not a hard counter. Gives users a chance to wrap up or shift modes before the wall.
MeasureReduction in mid-session limit interruptions. Pro user satisfaction score after limit events vs. control.
Tradeoff: Showing limits makes limits more visible. Short-term frustration risk. But hiding them until they hit is worse for trust. P0 because it directly impacts power user retention.
Bet 02
P1

Persistent Artifacts inside Projects

ProblemArtifacts built in Projects don't survive across sessions. Workspace context persists. Work output doesn't.
Why nowProjects and Artifacts are both mature features. The gap between them is the most obvious missing piece in the workspace story.
ShipAn Artifact library inside each Project — every Artifact saved, versioned, accessible in future sessions. One click to pull back into context.
MeasureArtifact reuse rate across sessions. Session depth for users with Artifact libraries vs. without.
Tradeoff: Adds storage and versioning complexity. P1 because it's high-impact for power users but requires non-trivial infra work.
Bet 03
P2

A Builder pricing tier

ProblemCurrent tiers aren't designed around the builder use case. A solo developer using API + MCP + extended thinking is a different user than a team using Claude for writing.
Why nowThe most valuable users hit the hardest limits. That's a retention and word-of-mouth problem.
ShipA Builder tier — higher limits on API, extended thinking, and MCP sessions, priced for individual builders not enterprise teams. Usage-based not hard-cap.
Measure90-day retention among Builder tier vs. Pro. API usage growth. NPS among power users who previously churned due to limits.
Tradeoff: Pricing complexity, support overhead. P2 because it's a business model change — harder to ship than a product feature but high long-term leverage.
06 — Bigger Pattern

What this tells me about AI products

The chatbot era is ending. Claude with Projects, Artifacts, MCP, and Skills is not really a chatbot anymore. It's an environment. That shift — from conversation to workspace — is the most important transition happening in AI products right now.

The depth-cost problem is the defining challenge. Any AI product that tries to serve serious users will hit this tension: the features that create real value use more compute, cost more, hit limits faster. How companies solve this will define which products win the serious user market. Claude's direction is right. The business model needs to catch up.

Product Teardown · Lovable

Lovable: winning the starting problem — and the risk that comes with it

It sells the feeling of "I can finally build my idea myself." That emotional hook is the real product.

📅 March 2026⏱ 2-min summary + full teardown✍️ By Janhvi
Executive Summary — 2 min read
1
Product thesis
Lovable isn't really an AI coding tool. It's an emotional product — it sells the feeling of "I can finally build my idea myself." For non-technical founders and PMs, it solves the starting problem better than anyone. But the same abstraction that makes it accessible is what makes it risky — AI-generated apps can look complete while having real issues underneath.
2
Target user
Non-technical founders, PMs, and designers who have product ideas but can't turn them into working software. People blocked by the starting problem — not the coding problem.
3
What works
  • Idea → live app in minutes — best in class at the emotional first moment
  • Full-stack abstraction — frontend, backend, auth, deploy in one flow
  • GitHub integration — code ownership reduces lock-in fear
4
What is broken
  • No production readiness guidance — users don't know what they're shipping
  • ICP tension — non-technical vs. technical-leaning builders want different things
  • Speed moat is fragile — v0, Bolt, Replit closing the gap fast
5
My PM bet
A "ready to ship?" checklist in the launch flow. Non-technical builders don't know what production-ready means. A pre-launch flow that checks for exposed API keys, missing input validation, and unauthenticated routes — in plain language, not error codes — closes the gap between "it works" and "it's ready to ship."
6
Metric I'd track
Apps deployed to production per month — not just apps created. Creation is table stakes. Deployment signals the user trusted Lovable enough to show their idea to real users. That trust is the product.
01 — Core Bet

Solve the starting problem, not the coding problem

The biggest mistake you can make when reading Lovable is thinking it's trying to be a better coding tool. It isn't. The problem it's solving is much more specific: many founders, PMs, designers, and non-technical builders have product ideas but struggle to turn those ideas into something live and usable. The starting problem. The gap between "I have an idea" and "I have something I can show someone."

"Lovable isn't selling code. It's selling the feeling of 'I can finally build my idea myself.' That emotional hook is the real product."

What Lovable does is abstract the hardest parts of early development — frontend, backend, database, auth, deployment — into a conversational flow. You describe what you want. It builds. You see something working. That loop is fast, and for someone who's never been able to touch a working product before, it's genuinely exciting.

02 — User Journey

How users actually experience Lovable

The first-time user journey is the product's strongest moment. You open Lovable, describe your idea in plain language — "a tool that helps freelancers track their invoices" — and within minutes you see a working app in the browser. The emotional beat of that first moment is powerful: this is something you could never have built before, and now it exists. That feeling is the hook.

The second-session journey is where the product gets complicated. You return to iterate, fix something, add a feature. The AI modifies the code. Sometimes it breaks something that was working. You prompt it to fix the break. It fixes it but breaks something else. For non-technical users, this loop can feel opaque — you can see symptoms but not the cause, and you don't have the vocabulary to diagnose precisely.

The launch journey is the biggest gap. You've built something, it looks done, you want to share it. But there's no "before you go live, here's what to check" flow. The app might have exposed API keys, accept any input without validation, or have routes that don't require authentication. The builder has no way of knowing. The journey ends at "it looks done" when it should end at "it's ready."

03 — Product Decisions

Five decisions that reveal the strategy

Decision 01

Conversational build flow — abstraction as the product

No setup. No config files. No choosing a framework. The entire product keeps the intimidation of starting as close to zero as possible. For a technical builder, hiding these things is frustrating — you lose control. For a non-technical founder, hiding these things is the entire value. Lovable is reading its user correctly here.

Abstraction as primary UX, not a shortcut
Decision 02

Speed from idea to live app — owning the emotional first moment

The thing Lovable does better than any competitor is compress the timeline from "I have an idea" to "I have something I can show." That compression is the emotional core. The first working version is when users form their attachment to a product. Lovable owns that moment better than anyone. The challenge is what happens next — when initial excitement meets real limitations.

Strongest competitive advantage
Decision 03

Full-stack generation — breadth over depth

Lovable generates frontend, backend, database, auth, and deployment together. Much more ambitious than v0 (component-level) or most other tools. The breadth is what makes the "idea → live app" promise real. But breadth creates a quality tradeoff — each layer gets less attention than a specialist tool. The app looks complete. The auth might have gaps. The database schema might not scale.

Breadth enables the promise · Depth is the outstanding debt
Decision 04

GitHub integration — code ownership as a trust signal

Lovable lets you connect to GitHub and own the generated code. This reduces the fear of lock-in. It also signals something about the evolving ICP: pure non-technical users don't care about GitHub. The integration tells me Lovable is also trying to serve startup founders who want to validate fast but know they'll hand code to engineers eventually.

Trust signal · And ICP signal
Decision 05

What Lovable hasn't built — the production gap

No built-in guidance around what you're actually shipping. No security review surface. No warning when a database schema choice might cause problems at scale. No flagging of missing input validation. For a non-technical builder who doesn't know what they don't know, this is the biggest gap. The app looks done. It might not be safe. And the builder has no way of knowing.

The gap between "it works" and "it's ready to ship"
04 — Competitors

A crowded but differentiated space

v0 (Vercel) is optimized for component-level UI generation — a tool for developers who know what they want and need it fast. Bolt is closer to Lovable in spirit but leans more technical, offering more control surfaces. Replit Agent is broader — collaborative coding, cloud environment, serving developers and learners more than non-technical founders. None of them occupy the same exact cell as Lovable: full-stack, non-technical, emotionally accessible. That distinctiveness is real. But speed is hard to hold as a moat — every competitor is working on the same problem with similar models. If Lovable closes the production readiness gap before competitors, that becomes a much more durable differentiator.

05 — What I'd Do Next

Three bets — with priority

Bet 01
P0

Ship readiness — a production checklist in the launch flow

ProblemNon-technical builders don't know what "production ready" means. They ship when it looks done, not when it's safe.
Why nowOne high-profile security incident with a Lovable-built app could damage trust across the entire user base. Getting ahead is cheaper than recovering.
ShipA "Ready to ship?" flow before deployment — checks exposed API keys, missing input validation, unauthenticated routes. Plain language, not error codes. Informative, not alarming.
Measure% of apps that pass checklist before going live. Support ticket volume related to security post-launch.
Tradeoff: Adds friction to the launch moment — Lovable's strongest emotional beat. Has to be designed carefully: confidence, not fear. P0 because one bad incident damages the trust foundation the whole product rests on.
Bet 02
P1

User type onboarding — two paths, two products

ProblemLovable is serving two users with different needs in the same flow. Non-technical founders want maximum abstraction. Technical-leaning builders want selective control. Neither is fully optimized.
Why nowAs Lovable scales, the ICP tension gets louder. Defining it deliberately through onboarding is cleaner than letting it fragment organically.
ShipTwo-question onboarding: "Are you technical?" + "What are you building?" Two paths — max abstraction for non-technical, more control surfaces for technical builders.
MeasureActivation rate by user type. 30-day retention per path vs. blended baseline.
Tradeoff: Splitting onboarding adds complexity. P1 because clarity for both user types has high leverage on retention.
Bet 03
P2

Developer handoff doc — own the transition moment

ProblemMany Lovable users will eventually hand their app to a developer. The developer gets a GitHub repo and has to figure out the rest — no context, no architecture overview, no known limitations.
Why nowThis transition is where Lovable apps either survive into production or get abandoned and rebuilt from scratch. Owning it well keeps the builder on Lovable longer.
ShipAuto-generated "developer handoff doc" — architecture overview, database schema explanation, env variable list, known limitations. Generated from the app itself, exportable as markdown.
Measure% of apps that reach handoff stage vs. total created. Developer NPS after receiving a handoff doc.
Tradeoff: Building documentation generation well is technically hard. P2 because P0 and P1 protect the core product first.
06 — Bigger Pattern

What this tells me about AI codegen

The starting problem is solved. The shipping problem isn't. Lovable and the entire AI codegen category have gotten very good at helping people start building. What nobody has fully solved yet is the "prototype → production-ready" loop. That's where the real product opportunity sits, and whichever tool solves it first will have a much more durable moat than speed.

Accessibility creates a new kind of technical debt. When non-technical builders ship AI-generated apps without understanding what's underneath, they create a new category of technical debt — not from bad engineering decisions, but from uninformed ones. The platforms enabling this have a responsibility to help users understand what they're shipping. Lovable is the most prominent tool in this category, which means this responsibility lands on them most visibly.

Decisions I Made

Key product decisions — and why

The full learning loop over more content
There's no shortage of AI courses and videos — that's exactly why learners are lost. So I chose not to build another content library. AI² owns the whole loop instead: what to learn, how to understand it, how to practice it, how to get feedback, and how to prove it. Content is the easy part. The loop is the product.
2
Outcomes over engagement
Most learning products optimize for time-in-app. I designed AI² around portfolio readiness and measurable skill instead. The goal isn't to keep a learner busy — it's to get them to clarity, completion, and proof. That's why the dashboard tracks weak areas and progress, not streaks and badges.
3
Evaluation rubrics at the core, not bolted on
It's easy to generate AI feedback. It's hard to know if a learner is actually improving. So I made evaluation a first-class part of the system — rubrics for quizzes, portfolio tasks, interview practice, and progress. Without that, AI feedback is just more text. With it, the product can actually tell when someone is ready to move on.
4
A structured companion, not a chatbot
A chatbot waits for you to know what to ask. A lost learner doesn't know what to ask. So AI² leads — it builds the path, decides what comes next, and guides the learner through it. The AI is the engine, but the structure is what makes it useful for someone who's confused about where to even start.

Decisions I Made

Key product decisions — and why

Every one of these came from the same place: farming is a high-stakes, low-margin-for-error category. The decisions reflect that.

1
RAG before fine-tuning
Farming advice needs trusted grounding, not just fluent answers. A fine-tuned model can sound confident and still be wrong. RAG lets me point every answer back to a real, trusted source — which matters far more here than fluency.
2
Structured advisory format over free text
Farmers need an action, a warning, and a source — not a long block of AI text. So I designed every response to return exactly that structure. It makes the guidance scannable, builds trust through transparency, and always ends with something the farmer can actually do.
3
Crop + district context from the start
Agricultural advice changes by region, season, and crop. Generic advice is close to useless on a real farm. So the farm profile — district, taluka, crop, soil, season — feeds into every advisory, instead of treating all farmers as the same user.
4
Evaluation before scale
Wrong farming advice can cause real financial damage — a bad spray decision or fertilizer dose costs money and crops. So I built evaluation rubrics for grounding and safety early, before chasing more features or more users. Getting the answers right matters more than getting big fast.

How I measure whether an advisory is good

Is the answer backed by a trusted source, or is the LLM guessing?
Safety
Does it avoid risky advice? Does it warn where caution is needed?
Usefulness
Does it give the farmer a clear, actionable next step?
Clarity
Is it in simple language a farmer can understand and act on?
